# Total Addressable Market & Ideal-Customer Fit

## What this is

A market-sizing tool that answers two questions leadership needs together, not separately: how big is the total market Acme could ever sell into, and which companies in it — customers and non-customers alike — are the best fit, worth going after first. It's built for GTM and executive leadership setting market strategy and investment priorities, not for reps working an individual account list.

## Why it exists

The metric tree tracks how the business is performing week to week against plan; this tool answers a different, longer-horizon question the tree was never built to answer — how big is the market overall, and where is the best-fit opportunity concentrated within it. That's a strategic, market-facing question rather than an operating-cadence one, which is why it sits outside the weekly scorecard entirely rather than feeding into it. Leadership needs a real, computed answer to "how big could this get" and "where should we point the next dollar of go-to-market investment" that's grounded in the same company-fit logic Acme already uses to qualify inbound leads, not a separately invented market model that could quietly disagree with it.

## How it works

Every known company in the market — whether or not it has ever bought from Acme — is placed into one of three fit tiers, using the exact same scoring Acme already uses to qualify inbound leads and decide which segment a new customer enters, just applied to the entire market instead of only the companies that happen to reach out. Tier 1 is the best fit for Acme's Enterprise motion, Tier 2 is a solid fit for the Commercial motion, and Tier 3 covers the self-serve SMB fit — which also automatically includes any company that signed up using a personal email address rather than a company domain, regardless of how it would otherwise have scored, since that's a reliable enough signal on its own that it overrides the rest of the fit calculation. Every company already captured as a customer is marked as such; everyone else counts as open whitespace. Each company, customer or not, is then assigned a typical deal value based on real, already-closed deals from other companies in its same fit tier — using the middle (median) deal size within the tier rather than the average, so a small number of unusually large deals don't set an inflated expectation applied across thousands of companies that haven't bought anything yet. For a company that's never transacted, the tier it would enter at is the only grounded signal available for what its first deal is likely to look like. Adding this up across the whole market — and breaking it out by region, industry, and company size — produces the total market size, how much of it is still open whitespace, and a ranked list of the highest-value companies still worth pursuing, prioritized by fit tier first and by fit strength within the tier second.

## Key decisions and why

- **ICP tiers reuse the exact same scoring and cut points that already qualify inbound leads and decide which segment a real customer enters, applied to the whole market rather than built as a separate scheme.** A market-sizing model that used a different fit definition than the one the business already uses to run go-to-market would leave leadership with two competing pictures of "who's a good fit" to reconcile, rather than one consistent answer.
- **A direct tier-based calculation was used instead of building a model to predict which specific non-customer companies are most likely to convert.** The real mechanism that decides which companies actually become customers is already known and explicit — better-fit companies within a tier convert more readily — so a predictive model trained to guess the same thing would mostly be re-deriving a rule that's already understood, rather than adding a genuinely new insight.
- **The typical deal value assigned to each tier is the median of real, already-closed deals, not the average.** Enterprise deal sizes in particular stretch out toward a small number of very large contracts; using the average would let those few large deals set an inflated per-company assumption that then gets applied across thousands of companies in the same tier that haven't bought anything.
- **Total market size and total addressable market are the same number at the company-count level — a genuine finding, confirmed against the data, not a shortcut.** Acme's go-to-market motion is deliberately built to cover the entire market by design: even the lowest-fit companies default into the self-serve SMB motion rather than being treated as unreachable, and every region, industry, and company-size band already has at least one real customer in it. A narrower "serviceable" market would require an explicit "this company can never be sold to" category that doesn't exist anywhere in how the business actually operates, so drawing one in would misstate the real go-to-market motion rather than describe it.
- **The total-market dollar figure is deliberately reported as a full-capture ceiling, with no discount applied for how likely a company actually is to convert.** Every company is valued at its tier's typical deal size as if it were already won — this project has no real, observed data on how a company actually converts from cold outreach the way it has real, observed win rates for deals already in active pipeline, so building in a conversion assumption here would mean inventing a number rather than grounding one in something real.
- **Which companies count as already-captured customers is evaluated as of a specific date, not treated as fixed.** A company's fit tier itself never changes — it reflects the market as it exists — but whether that company has become a customer yet is a moving fact, so every result is read as a snapshot as of a stated date rather than an average across time.
- **A company that becomes a customer and later churns still counts as captured, not as open whitespace again.** Treating a churned account as available market again would double-count it against a company that's never bought at all; winning back a lapsed customer is a real but distinct question this tool doesn't attempt to answer.

## What drives the result

- **Fit tier** — the same scoring and cut points already used to decide who enters Enterprise, Commercial, or SMB, applied market-wide: best-fit (Tier 1), solid-fit (Tier 2), and self-serve-fit (Tier 3)
- **The personal-email override** — any company that signed up with a personal rather than a company email address is automatically treated as Tier 3 (self-serve fit), regardless of its underlying score
- **Typical deal value per tier**, grounded in real closed-won new-business deals: roughly $1,800 for SMB-fit companies, roughly $15,000 for Commercial-fit companies, and roughly $237,700 for Enterprise-fit companies
- **Capture status** — whether a company has already become a customer as of the date being measured
- **Whitespace ranking** — companies not yet captured are prioritized by fit tier first, then by fit strength within that tier, matching the same pattern that already governs which companies genuinely become customers

## Current result

As of December 31, 2025, the market Acme could sell into totals 123,200 companies. Every one of them already falls within Acme's addressable go-to-market motion — every region, every industry, and every company-size band has at least one real customer — so there is no meaningfully narrower "serviceable" market to carve out separately from the total.

If every company in this market converted at its tier's typical deal size, the total ceiling is roughly $1.07 billion. That figure is explicitly a full-capture ceiling, not a realistic near-term forecast — no discount is applied for how likely a company actually is to convert, because there's no real data yet on how a company actually converts from cold outreach the way there is for deals already engaged in active pipeline. Of that total, roughly $1.03 billion sits in open whitespace across the 115,500 companies not yet captured — about 94% of the market is still open. The best-fit tier holds the largest open-dollar pool at roughly $487 million across 2,049 open companies, already the highest-penetrated of the three tiers at just over 5%. The solid-fit tier holds the second-largest open pool at roughly $385 million and is the least-penetrated tier of the three at under 1.5% of its population captured — a genuine finding worth acting on, not a modeling artifact. The self-serve tier has by far the largest number of open companies but the smallest open-dollar pool, at roughly $158 million, since each individual company in that tier is worth far less.

One useful negative finding worth knowing: among companies that entered as self-serve customers, their original fit score does not predict which of them later grow into a bigger segment — the rate of growing up a segment holds flat at roughly 10% regardless of how strong or weak the original fit score was. That's expected, not a flaw in the scoring: growing into a bigger segment is driven by how much a company actually uses the product after becoming a customer, a separate mechanism from the firmographic fit that decides which segment it enters in the first place. In practical terms, this tool is a reliable guide to how big a company's first deal is likely to be — not to which customers will organically grow the fastest afterward.

## Known limitations

- **The total-market and whitespace dollar figures are a full-capture ceiling, not a forecast of what will actually close.** No conversion-rate or win-rate discount is applied to companies that haven't yet become customers — there's no real, observed data in this project yet that grounds how a cold, unengaged company actually converts, the way there is real data grounding win rates for deals already in active pipeline.
- **A company that becomes a customer and later churns is not counted back as open whitespace.** Of the companies ever captured as customers, a meaningful share have since churned, and all of them still count as captured rather than available. Sizing the win-back opportunity for lapsed customers is a distinct question this tool doesn't attempt.
- **Fit score predicts the right deal size at entry, not which customers will organically grow into a bigger segment afterward.** As the negative finding above shows, growth after entry is driven by real usage, not by the original fit score — a tool aiming to prioritize whitespace by "likely to grow fast once won" would need a different signal than this one provides.
- **Geographic sales-territory assignment is not used here, even though the underlying data now has a field for it.** That field exists to support a separate, not-yet-built tool focused specifically on territory coverage and account routing; folding it into this market-sizing view would blur two different questions that are better kept apart.
- **There is no narrower "serviceable" market carved out beneath the total market figure.** That's a genuine reflection of how Acme's go-to-market motion is built — no company in the market is structurally out of reach — but it also means this tool currently carries less nuance than it would if a real "cannot be sold to" category existed. If the business ever adds a genuine floor below which even the self-serve motion doesn't apply, this finding would need to be revisited rather than assumed to still hold.
