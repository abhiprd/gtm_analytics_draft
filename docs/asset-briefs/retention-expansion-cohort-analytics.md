# Cohort Retention & Expansion Analysis

## What this is

A time-lapse view of how customers actually behave after they sign up, built by grouping every account into a "cohort" based on the quarter it joined and the segment it entered in, then watching that same group over time. It answers a question a single snapshot of today's customer base can't: does the shape of a customer's retention curve match what its contract structure should predict? It's built for GTM leadership, Customer Success, and Account Management to see retention as a genuine time-lapse of each signup class, not a point-in-time read of the whole book blended together.

## Why it exists

The metric tree's segment-level logo retention and the company's revenue-retention metrics describe the business as it stands today, blended across customers of every age and tenure. That blended view can hide a pattern that only shows up when a single signup class is followed forward in time: whether a customer's odds of staying match the commitment they signed up for. This analysis exists to make that pattern visible on purpose, and to confirm the retention differences the business expects by segment — SMB lowest, Enterprise highest — are actually showing up in the data, not just assumed.

## How it works

Every customer account is placed into a cohort based on two things fixed at the moment it signed up: the three-month period it joined in, and which segment — SMB, Commercial, or Enterprise — it entered as. A cohort's membership is locked at that point; no customer joins a cohort after its first month, no matter what happens to it later. From there, each cohort is tracked forward using elapsed time since joining rather than calendar time: how many of its original members are still active customers at one month in, two months in, and so on, and how much combined monthly revenue the group is generating at each of those points relative to its very first month. A churning customer isn't marked with a flag — its records simply stop appearing, which is how an exit shows up in the analysis. The same curves are also pooled two other ways: combining every cohort within a segment regardless of when it joined, and combining every cohort by the channel that first brought the customer in — giving one overall retention shape per segment and per channel, in addition to each individual signup class's own curve.

## Key decisions and why

- **A customer's segment at the moment it signed up is used to build cohorts, not its segment today.** An account that later grows and moves up into a bigger segment is still counted in the cohort it started in, so this analysis shows what actually happened to each starting class over time rather than reshuffling accounts into the group they eventually became.
- **An account that grows into a bigger segment stays a full member of its original cohort, and its higher revenue after that move counts toward its cohort's revenue trajectory.** A cohort here is a group of specific customers followed over time, so a member growing — even by moving into a new segment — is still that same customer's growth, not something to carve out.
- **Cohorts are built on a three-month (quarterly), not monthly, basis, applied evenly across all three segments.** Signup volume for Commercial and especially Enterprise customers is too thin in any single month to produce a stable curve, and using one consistent time window for every segment keeps the three directly comparable rather than mixing different levels of precision. Where a particular quarter's group is still small, that's shown plainly alongside the number rather than hidden.
- **A structural, descriptive summary was used instead of a predictive model.** The purpose here is to describe what has already happened to a fixed, closed group of customers, not to forecast what will happen to an open, still-changing population — that predictive, individual-customer question is already answered by the account health score, a separate and purpose-built tool. Reusing that same modeling approach here would blur two genuinely different jobs together.
- **Retention is counted two different ways depending on whether a single signup class or a whole segment is being viewed.** For one specific signup quarter's group, every member is held in the denominator for as long as the whole group has collectively been observed, so the count never quietly shrinks as time passes. For a whole segment or channel pooled across many signup quarters of different ages, the denominator instead only includes customers old enough to have reached that point in time, shrinking as elapsed time grows — the standard way to combine groups of different ages without throwing away everything a mature signup class can show just because a newer one hasn't caught up yet.
- **The comparison against the company's own benchmark retention ranges is checked three years after signup, not one year after.** Commercial customers are on annual contracts and Enterprise customers are on multi-year contracts, so checking at the one-year mark would show both segments sitting at an artificially perfect number — neither has reached its first real renewal decision yet. Three years out is the earliest point at which every segment has had a genuine chance to churn, so the comparison reflects real retention behavior rather than an unexpired-contract illusion.
- **The raw ratio used to describe revenue retention is reported as a trend, not measured against a numeric target.** Because usage (and therefore revenue) naturally ramps up over a customer's early months, a brand-new signup's very first month of revenue is small compared to where it settles once fully onboarded — so early revenue-retention ratios read as sharply inflated purely from normal ramp-up, not from genuine, ongoing expansion. Grading that number against a fixed target would risk mistaking healthy onboarding growth for either an outsized win or a miscalibration.
- **Revenue retention is split into how much comes from customers simply staying versus how much comes from surviving customers spending more over time**, rather than left as one blended ratio. Separating the two lets a reader tell "we kept the logos" apart from "the logos we kept are worth more now," instead of a single number that could mean either.

## What drives the result

- **Signup quarter and the segment a customer entered in** — fixed at the moment of signup and never re-assigned, defining which cohort a customer belongs to
- **Time elapsed since signup**, not calendar date — the axis every curve is measured against, so cohorts that joined years apart can be compared on equal footing
- **Whether the customer still shows up in the company's monthly revenue records** — presence, not a flag, is how staying vs. leaving is tracked
- **Contract structure by segment** — SMB is month-to-month with no contract term, Commercial is on an annual contract, and Enterprise is on a multi-year contract; this isn't a literal input to the calculation, but it's the reason the resulting curve shapes look the way they do
- **Acquisition channel** — the other cut the analysis runs, checked separately from segment since how an account was acquired and what segment it is are treated as unrelated facts about it

## Current result

As of December 31, 2025, the pattern leadership would expect from contract structure shows up clearly and matches it: SMB customers, who can leave in any given month because nothing locks them in, show a smooth, steady erosion starting in their very first month as a customer. Commercial customers, who are on annual contracts, instead sit at essentially 100% retention for their entire first year, then drop sharply right at the one-year mark — 86.5% retained by month 13 — exactly where their first renewal decision lands. Enterprise customers, on multi-year contracts, hold flat near 100% for two full years and then drop at month 25 (92.5% retained) at their first multi-year renewal point. In other words, a contract-locked cohort's numbers can look artificially perfect right up until its first renewal comes due — "retention looks great" for a young Commercial or Enterprise cohort isn't the full story until that cohort has lived past its first renewal date.

Checked three years after signup and translated to an annual rate, all three segments land inside the company's own benchmark ranges, in the expected order: SMB at 83.8% (against a 75–85% range), Commercial at 92.0% (against 88–93%), and Enterprise at 97.0% (against 93–97%) — SMB lowest, Enterprise highest, exactly as expected, with genuine room between the three rather than a coincidental near-tie.

Acquisition channel shows no meaningful difference in how well customers stick around: at the one-year mark, self-serve and inbound-marketing customers retain almost identically (86.4% and 86.5%), and while sales-outbound-sourced customers show 100% retention at that same point, that reading comes from a very small group of accounts and isn't a reliable signal either way. This is a useful finding in its own right — how a customer was acquired doesn't appear to move its odds of staying, unlike segment, which moves it substantially.

Revenue retention behaves as expected for a usage-based product with a ramp-up period: early ratios run well above 100% of a cohort's first-month revenue (Commercial cohorts run roughly 12 times their month-0 revenue by the one-year mark, SMB roughly 8 times by six months in) — a sign that a brand-new signup's first month understates its steady-state spend, not a sign of unusually strong expansion.

## Known limitations

- A handful of the earliest Enterprise cohorts, and some early Commercial cohorts, are built from a small number of accounts given how few Enterprise and Commercial customers sign up in any given quarter. Those thin groups are shown with their size alongside every figure rather than smoothed over, but they should be read as directional rather than precise for those specific quarters.
- Revenue-retention ratios in the early months are dominated by normal usage ramp-up rather than genuine expansion, for the reason described above, and are reported as a trend to watch rather than a number graded against a target.
- The channel comparison shows no clear difference between how self-serve, marketing-sourced, and sales-outbound customers retain, but the sales-outbound group is small enough that a real advantage in either direction can't be confidently ruled in or out from this data.
- This analysis doesn't check whether an account's relationship-management ownership actually hands off between teams as it ages or moves segments, or reconcile its own revenue figures directly against the company's segment-migration tracking — the two look at customers from genuinely different angles and aren't intended to be checked against each other's specific numbers.
