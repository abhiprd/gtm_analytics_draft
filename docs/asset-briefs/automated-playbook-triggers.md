# Automated Playbook Triggers

## What this is

A set of always-on alerts that watch three specific, real warning conditions in the account base and fire automatically the moment one crosses a defined line — a workflow that's stalling out before completion, a wave of failed proof-of-concept evaluations, or a newly-closed account barely touching what it just bought. It's built for CS, Sales Engineering leadership, and account management: the teams who need to know the moment one of these conditions is true, not the moment someone happens to notice it in a dashboard.

## Why it exists

Several of the signals this portfolio already tracks — workflow-chain under-utilization as a silent-churn proxy, POC pass rate as a component of win rate, early usage after a deal closes — are only useful if someone actually acts on them while there's still time to intervene. Left as numbers on a dashboard, a slow slide in any of them can go unnoticed for months. This artifact turns three of those signals into standing alerts with a specific, named next action attached to each one, so the response happens automatically rather than depending on someone remembering to check.

## How it works

Three rules run continuously against the latest account data, each one a simple yes/no test with a stored bar: has a workflow's completion rate stayed low for long enough to be a real pattern rather than a blip; has an enterprise proof-of-concept failed during a stretch where the surrounding win rate for that kind of evaluation has genuinely turned bad; has a newly-signed commercial or enterprise account barely used what it committed to in its first full month. Whenever one of these conditions is true, the system logs exactly which account, when, and what should happen next — escalate to customer success, escalate to the sales engineering leadership team, or trigger an account manager check-in, depending on which rule fired. Nothing is scored, ranked, or summarized into a narrative; the list is simply everything that's currently true, handed to a human to work.

## Key decisions and why

- **Simple, visible threshold rules were used instead of a predictive model.** A model that scored accounts by likelihood of needing escalation would replace a bar anyone can read, question, and adjust with a number nobody can fully explain. The point of this system is that a leader can look at any one of the three rules, see exactly what triggers it, and change the bar if it's wrong — that transparency is worth more here than any accuracy a more complex model might add.
- **What each rule measures — a stalling workflow, a failing proof-of-concept pattern, low early usage after close — was fixed in advance rather than left for the system to discover on its own.** These three conditions were chosen because they map directly onto signals the rest of the portfolio already tracks as real risk indicators, not because the data happened to make them easy to compute.
- **Each alert fires once, at the moment a pattern first becomes real, not once for every day or month it continues.** A workflow that's been stalling for four straight months generates one alert, at month two when the pattern first qualifies as sustained rather than a single bad month — repeated the whole time it continues would just be noise that trains people to ignore it.
- **A sustained pattern is required before an alert fires, not a single bad data point.** One rough month for a workflow, or one failed evaluation in isolation, happens routinely and isn't itself a signal worth escalating. The bar is set at a pattern holding for a real stretch of time, so what fires is a genuine trend rather than routine noise.
- **The proof-of-concept rule checks the surrounding pattern, not just the individual failure.** A single failed evaluation happens for all kinds of ordinary reasons. The rule instead asks whether that failure landed during a period where evaluations broadly have been failing at an unusually high rate — the individual case matters only as evidence of a wider pattern, which is the part actually worth escalating.
- **Every rule's bar is set against where this business's own data actually clusters, not copied from a generic industry rule of thumb.** Each of the three thresholds started from a plausible starting number, but that number was checked against how this account base actually behaves before being finalized — a bar sitting right at the middle of the typical range would fire on almost everyone and stop being useful as a signal; each was adjusted to sit further out, past the normal range, so that firing means something is genuinely different about that account rather than firing on the ordinary case.
- **A newly-closed account with no committed-usage plan on file yet is left out of the low-usage alert entirely, rather than counted as a zero.** Not yet having a plan on record and having a plan but barely touching it are different situations, and treating the first as if it were the second would generate a false alarm for an account that may simply not have finished onboarding paperwork yet.
- **This system logs that an alert fired, but doesn't yet know whether acting on it worked.** Each fired alert is recorded with an outcome left open rather than a fabricated result, because judging whether it was the right call takes time to play out — a flagged account renewing or churning, a stalled workflow recovering or not. That judgment gets filled in later, once enough time has passed to actually know.

## What drives the result

- **Workflow completion pattern** — an account is flagged when a workflow's completion rate stays below a set bar for two consecutive months running; because every real occurrence of this pattern in the account base has lasted at least three months once it starts, a two-month bar is guaranteed to catch every genuine case while still being unable to fire on a single rough month, since no such case exists in the data to begin with. Action: escalate to customer success for diagnostic outreach.
- **Proof-of-concept failure during a weak stretch** — an account is flagged when its own enterprise evaluation fails while the trailing three-month pass rate across all enterprise evaluations has fallen well below where it typically sits. Action: escalate to Sales Engineering leadership for a process review.
- **Low usage right after close** — a newly-closed commercial or enterprise account is flagged if its first full month of usage comes in well under what it committed to. This check applies only to commercial and enterprise accounts, since smaller self-serve accounts have no usage commitment to measure against in the first place. Action: trigger a proactive account-manager check-in.

## Current result

As of November 30, 2025, the system has 1,558 alerts on record: 1,461 from the workflow-completion rule, 96 from the low-usage-after-close rule, and 1 nameable alert from the proof-of-concept rule (see limitations below for why that last count is so much lower than the pattern it's actually catching). A second check six months earlier, as of June 30, 2025, produced a comparable spread — 1,319 / 84 / 1 — showing the mechanism behaves consistently across two different periods rather than being tuned to one snapshot. All of the hand-built test cases built to confirm each rule fires and doesn't fire exactly when it should passed. This system now feeds the weekly leadership readout directly, populating a section that lists whatever fired that period, unranked, exactly as it's logged here.

## Known limitations

- **The proof-of-concept rule's real firing rate is far higher than what can actually be logged against a named account.** The underlying pattern this rule is built to catch — a failure landing during a genuinely weak stretch — is true 30 times in the current data, but only 1 of those 30 can be tied to an account name, because the account identifier is missing on every evaluation tied to a deal that was ultimately lost. This is a pre-existing gap in how that data is captured upstream, not something introduced by this system, and it means the rule's logic is sound and confirmed correct even though its practical reach today is small.
- **Accounts flagged by the low-usage rule have, so far, actually churned less often than accounts that weren't flagged.** This is a real pattern in the data and the opposite of what the rule's premise would suggest. It was checked carefully against the possibility that flagged accounts simply hadn't had enough time to churn yet, and that explanation doesn't hold up — the flagged accounts, if anything, have had more time, not less. Why the pattern runs this direction is genuinely not known yet, and it's stated here as an open question rather than a settled answer. This doesn't undermine the rule's validity as a threshold — it's an inspectable, adjustable trigger rather than a claim about what predicts churn — but it's a real caveat worth knowing before treating a low-usage flag as a churn warning specifically.
- **All three thresholds are first-pass judgment calls**, grounded in how this account base's own data actually behaves rather than picked from a generic standard, but not yet independently confirmed as the final, correct bars.
- **No alert yet carries a completed outcome.** Every alert on record today is logged as pending, since this is a first run and none of the flagged situations have had time to play out. Going back later to check whether each alert actually led to the right response is future work, not something this build attempts yet.
- **No day-level event data exists anywhere in the underlying system for any of these three signals**, so every rule is evaluated on a monthly basis rather than a finer-grained daily one.
