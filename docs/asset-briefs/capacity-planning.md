# Sales Capacity Planning

## What this is

A check on whether a sales team's assigned target is actually achievable given the real makeup of its roster — not just how many people are on it. It's built for sales leadership and RevOps to answer a sharper question than headcount planning normally asks: two teams the same size can have genuinely different real capacity to sell, depending on how many of their reps are still new and ramping up versus fully up to speed.

## Why it exists

The metric tree names rep capacity and ramp mix as a real driver of win rate, and quota-setting itself only works if the number assigned to a rep reflects what a rep in their position can actually produce. A target set without accounting for how much of the team is still ramping looks the same on paper as one that accounts for it, but only one of them is honest about what the team can deliver. This tool exists to turn "is this team's number realistic" from a debate into a computed, decomposed answer, so a quota shortfall can be correctly attributed to the target being too high, to reps not being on seat long enough, or specifically to how many reps are new — rather than treated as one undifferentiated miss.

## How it works

For every rep who carries a real, dollar-denominated sales target — the new-business sales roles (Inside Sales reps who sell into Commercial accounts, and Account Executives who sell into Enterprise), since account managers who handle renewals and expansion aren't measured against a quota in this business — the tool looks at each complete quarter that rep was active, compares what they were assigned to sell against what they actually closed, and adjusts the comparison for how long they'd actually been selling and how experienced they were. A rep still in their first two quarters on the job is credited with only about half of what a fully up-to-speed rep in the same role is expected to produce, matching how the business actually plans for new hires ramping up, and a rep who only joined partway through a quarter is credited only for the portion of the quarter they were actually on the team. Adding this up by team and by quarter produces a real, achievable-capacity figure that can be compared against the team's assigned target, and any gap between the two is broken into exactly how much of it comes from the target itself being set too high, how much from lost selling time, and how much specifically from having too many new reps in the mix.

## Key decisions and why

- **A direct calculation was used instead of a fitted statistical model.** The ramp effect this tool accounts for is a known, fixed rule the business already plans around, not a pattern to be inferred statistically from data — and the question leadership actually needs answered ("does this team's total capacity cover its total target") is a straightforward sum, which a statistical model would only approximate rather than answer exactly. The number of new-rep quarters of real history available is also too thin to support a reliable team-by-team statistical model, so adding up what actually happened is the more stable read of the same data.
- **Only quota-bearing, new-business sales roles are covered, by design.** Quota itself is only a meaningful concept for the roles the business actually assigns dollar targets to; account managers' capacity is a real and related question, but it's already answered elsewhere in the portfolio (through book size, ramp status, and check-in cadence) rather than forced into a quota-achievability framing it was never built to have.
- **The ramp-period discount used in this tool — new reps credited at roughly half of a fully ramped rep's output for their first two quarters — is checked directly against what ramping reps actually produced, not just assumed.** Measured against real outcomes, ramping reps produced 48.8%–51.5% of a fully-ramped rep's output depending on the period measured, confirming the assumption is a genuine description of this business's ramp curve rather than an untested guess.
- **A rep's ramp status is evaluated at the midpoint of each quarter, not the start.** Evaluating at the start of the quarter would make the "first two quarters" ramp window last two quarters for a rep hired on day one but three quarters for a rep hired at quarter-end. Using the midpoint makes the ramp window land on exactly two quarters no matter when in the quarter a rep actually started.
- **Quota targets are grounded in what the business's own pipeline can realistically produce — real deal-closing volume, win rate, and average deal size — rather than set as a figure independent of actual selling capacity.** This is why a fully-ramped, fully-staffed rep's target is set at what a rep in that position genuinely closes, and why realistic company-wide attainment lands in the mid-80s to mid-90s percent range: a target set at real capacity is one that's genuinely achievable and genuinely missable, which is what makes attainment worth tracking at all.
- **The gap between a team's target and its real capacity is split into three pieces that add up exactly to the total gap**, rather than reported as one undifferentiated shortfall: how much comes from the target itself sitting above what even a fully-ramped team could produce, how much comes from lost selling time (reps joining or leaving mid-quarter), and how much comes specifically from having new, still-ramping reps in the mix. Splitting the gap this way is what lets leadership tell a "the target is set too high" problem apart from a "too many new reps hired at once" problem, instead of treating every miss the same way.
- **A team-quarter's capacity is only reported once enough real history exists to support a reliable estimate.** Where that history isn't there yet, the tool reports "not enough data yet" rather than a number — a team with too little track record could otherwise appear to have literally zero capacity to sell, which would be a false and misleading reading rather than an honest gap.
- **Two different comparisons are reported side by side on purpose.** One compares the team's assigned target directly against its real achievable capacity — the plain answer to "can this team hit its number." The other isolates just the ramp-mix effect on its own, in a way that stays accurate even if the overall target level shifts — this is the one that specifically answers "is a ramp-heavy roster the reason," separate from any question about whether the target itself is set correctly.
- **Every result here is checked against hard, exact arithmetic ties to the underlying closed-deal records, and against direct evidence that the ramp effect is real in the data rather than assumed.** Because nothing in this tool is a statistical estimate, there's no accuracy percentage to report — instead, its numbers are checked to make sure they reconcile exactly to what reps actually closed, and that ramping reps genuinely produce measurably less than fully-ramped reps before that discount is ever applied to a capacity figure.

## What drives the result

- **Which reps are covered** — only new-business sales roles with a real quota: Inside Sales reps (selling into Commercial accounts) and Account Executives (selling into Enterprise accounts)
- **Assigned target vs. actual closed business**, compared quarter by quarter for each rep
- **Ramp status** — a rep is considered still-ramping for their first two quarters on the job, and fully ramped after that
- **Ramp-period output discount** — a still-ramping rep is credited with roughly half of a fully-ramped rep's expected output
- **Selling-time adjustment** — a rep who joined or left partway through a quarter is only credited for the share of the quarter they were actually active
- **Achievability margin** — a team's target is only flagged as a real concern when it exceeds real achievable capacity by more than roughly 15%, a threshold set to avoid flagging ordinary noise while still catching genuine gaps

## Current result

As of December 2025, company-wide quota attainment runs 84.4% across the full simulated history and 94.8% over the years the sales pipeline has run at full, mature volume (2023 onward) — a realistic range where reps genuinely beat and genuinely miss their number, rather than a target no one could reach even under ideal conditions. Looking rep-by-rep across full years worked, the typical (median) rep lands at 75.7% of quota, the average is 87.0%, and just over a third of rep-years hit or exceeded 100%.

Comparing each team's assigned target directly against its real capacity, 34 of the 48 team-quarters with genuine business volume show the target exceeding capacity by some margin — driven mainly by the ramp effect and, in the earliest years, by a sales pipeline that hadn't yet reached full maturity. Isolating just the ramp-mix effect specifically — independent of whatever the overall target level is — narrows that to 13 of 48 team-quarters, and every one of them falls in the 2020–2022 build-out period, when new reps made up as much as 27–64% of a team's target in a given quarter. Not one team-quarter from 2023 onward breaches this ramp-specific threshold: every team since then carries a much smaller share of new reps and stays comfortably within a healthy margin.

All five of the tool's own validation checks pass, including one that reconciles its totals exactly against the underlying closed-deal records, and one confirming directly that ramping reps really do produce measurably less than fully-ramped reps in the real data, rather than the discount being an untested assumption.

## Known limitations

- **The achievability read for the earliest years is partly a reflection of the data window, not just the roster.** The target level is grounded in years when the sales pipeline was running at full, steady volume, then applied evenly back across the earlier build-out period — a stretch when the pipeline itself hadn't yet ramped up. That's a real part of why more team-quarters in that earlier window show a gap, and it's worth reading alongside the roster-composition story rather than instead of it.
- **This tool only covers the "how many reps are new" half of fully-loaded rep cost.** No rep compensation or cost data exists anywhere in the underlying system, so the dollar-cost side of a ramping roster — not just its capacity impact — isn't computable here.
- **The Inside Sales team's ramp penalty looks steeper in the real data than the Enterprise team's, but both are held to the same 50% discount.** There isn't yet enough ramping-rep history for Inside Sales specifically to justify giving that team its own separate discount, so the shared figure is used for both until more history accumulates.
- **This tool looks backward, not forward.** It assesses quarters that have already closed; it doesn't yet project what a team's capacity will look like next quarter under a planned hiring push, since that would require a hiring plan as an input, which isn't available anywhere in the underlying data.
