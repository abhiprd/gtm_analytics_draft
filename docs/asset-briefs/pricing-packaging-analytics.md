# Pricing & Packaging Analytics

## What this is

An answer to three separate questions pricing and revenue leadership routinely need checked: how deep are deals actually being discounted off list price, by segment, and does that match how each segment is supposed to be sold; does discounting more actually buy a better outcome (more wins, fewer losses to competitors); and are the usage minimums baked into Commercial and Enterprise contracts sized sensibly against what those accounts really consume. It's built for whoever owns pricing strategy, discount-approval policy, and contract packaging.

## Why it exists

The metric tree's sales branch names realized deal size at close — discount rate against list price and how deal size is trending within a segment's normal range — as a real driver worth tracking, not just a byproduct of individual negotiations. Separately, Commercial and Enterprise contracts are built around a committed usage minimum with overage billing on top, which is one of the mechanisms that drives expansion revenue over the life of a contract. Both pieces only matter to leadership as evidence, not intention: this asset is what turns "our pricing motion is supposed to work this way" and "our packaging is supposed to be sized this way" into a checked, current answer rather than an assumption.

## How it works

For every closed deal, the tool compares the price actually paid against the original list price to see how deep the real discount ran, broken out by segment and by whether the deal was new business, a renewal, or an expansion. It then checks whether deals that closed as wins carried meaningfully different discounts than deals that were lost, and separately whether deals lost specifically to a competitor were discounted differently than deals lost for other reasons — using a statistical test that repeatedly reshuffles which deals count as "won" and "lost" to see how often a gap this size would show up purely by chance, which is a more honest way to judge a real-versus-coincidental gap than eyeballing the raw numbers. Alongside that, for every Commercial and Enterprise account, it tracks how much of the account's committed monthly usage minimum was actually used, month by month, and checks whether that pattern is landing where the pricing design intends it to land. Finally, it looks at whether the typical size of a new deal, within its segment, has been trending up, down, or holding steady over the past year compared to the year before.

## Key decisions and why

- **A direct, decision-by-decision comparison against known pricing rules is used instead of a fitted statistical model, because there's a fixed, correct answer to check against rather than a pattern to infer.** Each segment's pricing motion — SMB's no-negotiation metered pricing, Commercial's negotiated annual contract, Enterprise's negotiated multi-year commitment — is a stated business rule with an expected discount range attached to it, not something to be learned from the data. Checking the real numbers directly against that stated rule is the accurate approach; fitting a model to re-derive a rule that's already known would add uncertainty rather than remove it.
- **The specific discount ranges each segment is checked against, and the usage-minimum sizing target packaging is measured against, are fixed by how pricing and contracts are actually designed for this business — not chosen or tuned by this tool.** SMB's near-zero discount band, Commercial's and Enterprise's wider negotiated bands, and the roughly 65%-of-expected-usage minimum that Commercial and Enterprise contracts are built around are all inputs this tool reads and checks against, not judgment calls it makes on its own.
- **A statistical test that directly checks "does a relationship exist" was used instead of building a predictive model that estimates how much winning a deal depends on its discount.** Building a model to predict outcomes from discount depth would produce a coefficient that looks meaningful even when the underlying relationship is essentially flat — which is exactly what's being checked for here. Testing directly for whether a real gap exists is the more honest tool when the leading hypothesis is that there may be no relationship to model in the first place.
- **Before trusting the "no relationship" result on discounting, the same test was run on a driver already known to matter — a deal's forecast confidence category — to confirm the test can actually detect a real, large effect when one exists.** That check showed an enormous, obvious gap in win rate across confidence categories, which is what makes the flat result on discount depth a genuine finding rather than a sign the test simply isn't sensitive enough to pick anything up.
- **Discount realization and packaging calibration are measured as an all-time snapshot as of each check date, rather than over a short recent window.** Both are read as a steady state — "how discounting and usage minimums typically run" — rather than as a trend, and a short window on the smaller segments would risk mistaking ordinary month-to-month noise for a real shift. Deal size, by contrast, is the one genuinely time-based question here, so it's checked as a rolling year against the year before it specifically to catch real drift.
- **Packaging calibration is checked against the usage level the pricing design actually intends, not against a plain "usage should roughly equal the minimum" assumption.** Committed minimums are deliberately set below expected usage so that going over the minimum is a normal, planned part of how revenue grows on these contracts — so "well calibrated" here means tracking close to that intended overage level, not clustering around the minimum itself.
- **The bar for flagging a real move in typical deal size is set wider than the bar used for the company's overall weekly numbers, because the sample behind any single segment's year-over-year deal-size read is much smaller than a company-wide figure.** A tighter bar tuned to the company-wide cadence would end up flagging ordinary sampling noise on the smaller segments as if it were a real shift.

## What drives the result

- **Segment and deal type** — SMB, Commercial, and Enterprise, each checked separately, and within each segment, new business, renewal, and expansion deals are checked separately too
- **List price versus the price actually paid** — the basis for every discount-depth read
- **Win/loss outcome, and specifically whether a loss was to a named competitor** — what discount depth is checked against for a real relationship
- **Monthly committed usage minimum versus actual usage** — tracked for every Commercial and Enterprise account (SMB has no committed minimum, since it's billed purely on metered usage with no contract)
- **The packaging design target** — minimums are deliberately set at roughly 65% of an account's expected usage, so a healthily calibrated account is expected to run at around 150% of its committed minimum, not right at 100% of it
- **Rolling twelve-month windows** for the deal-size trend read, compared against the twelve months immediately before

## Current result

As of November 30, 2025 (a second check on June 30, 2025 shows the same picture):

**Discount depth matches how each segment is actually sold.** SMB deals run at roughly 2.5% off list on average — consistent with a self-serve motion that doesn't really negotiate. Commercial deals run at roughly 12.5% off, and Enterprise deals run at roughly 20% off, sometimes closer to 21% on expansion deals — both consistent with negotiated, multi-year or annual contracts where deeper give is expected. All of this sits inside the range each segment's pricing motion is designed to produce, and the depth of discount doesn't meaningfully differ between new business, renewal, and expansion deals within a segment.

**Deeper discounts don't buy a better outcome.** Comparing won deals to lost deals, and competitive losses to other kinds of losses, the gap in average discount is under half a percentage point in every case checked, and none of it clears the bar for a real, statistically meaningful difference. The same test run on a known real driver (a deal's forecast confidence category) shows an enormous, obvious gap in win rate — confirming the test is capable of finding a real signal when one exists, which makes the flat result on discounting a genuine finding rather than a blind spot in the check itself. The practical takeaway: reps who discount more aren't measurably buying better win rates or fewer competitive losses on this evidence, so tightening discount-approval policy toward the tighter end of each segment's normal range would not, on what this data shows, cost win rate.

**Committed usage minimums are tracking close to their intended target, with one honest caveat about how recent the reading is.** Commercial accounts are using roughly 149% of their committed minimum on a typical month, and Enterprise accounts roughly 155% — both close to the roughly 150% level the pricing design is actually built around, since minimums are deliberately set below expected usage. The large majority of account-months (around 85%) run well past the minimum, which is the packaging design working as intended, not a sign accounts are under-sized. The one caveat: this reading blends the full multi-year history together, including the earliest, thinnest years when the customer base was small and usage patterns hadn't yet settled. Looking at just the most recent full year on its own, Enterprise usage is already running somewhat above this blended average — a reminder that the combined, full-history number moves more slowly than the current book actually is, and could be a step behind if that more recent pattern continues.

**Typical deal size is holding steady.** Comparing the most recent twelve months of new-business deals to the twelve months before that, every segment's move is small — a few percent up or down at most — well inside the range treated as ordinary variation rather than a real drift. Every single new-business deal also falls within its segment's normal size range, with no exceptions.

## Known limitations

- **The finding that discount depth doesn't affect outcomes describes how this pricing motion currently behaves in the underlying data, not a general claim about discounting anywhere.** It's reported as a real, checked result — not a deeper truth about sales psychology — and if the underlying dynamics between discounting and winning ever genuinely change, this same check would pick that change up directly the next time it runs.
- **The packaging-calibration read is a segment-level pattern, not an account-by-account one.** It shows whether Commercial and Enterprise usage minimums are sized sensibly for the segment as a whole; it doesn't identify which specific accounts are furthest above or below their own commitment. That is a narrower, account-level question a related check is built to answer separately.
- **There's no cost or margin data behind this, so the finding about usage minimums is stated in usage terms, not dollar terms.** It can show that the large majority of account-months rely on usage above the committed minimum, but it can't currently state what share of actual billed revenue comes from the minimum itself versus the overage on top of it — that would need billed-dollar data this tool doesn't have access to today.
