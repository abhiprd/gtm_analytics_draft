# Marketing Attribution & Channel Mix

## What this is

An analysis that finally breaks marketing's contribution to new-customer pipeline into its three real parts — organic content, paid advertising, and community/events — instead of one blended "marketing" number, and shows two different, equally important answers to "which channel is actually working": which channel a converting customer's touch history suggests deserves credit, and which channel's marketing genuinely caused more customers to convert when it was tested directly. It's built for marketing and revenue leadership deciding where channel budget and attention should go.

## Why it exists

The growth framework this company reports against has always called for marketing pipeline to be split into organic, paid, and community — each with its own set of sub-metrics — but until this analysis existed, nothing computed that split; it existed only as a defined structure with no numbers behind it. This closes that gap using the individual touchpoint records — every website visit, ad click, event registration, and similar interaction — that are tracked for each prospect. It also closes a related blind spot on the efficiency side: the cost of acquiring a customer through marketing has only ever been available as one blended figure, which is enough to say marketing is getting more expensive but not which specific channel is responsible. This analysis exposes that at the sub-channel level for the first time.

## How it works

Every individual touchpoint a prospect has with marketing — a website visit, an ad click, an event registration — is tracked and tagged with the sub-channel that produced it. When a prospect eventually becomes a paying customer, this analysis looks back across that person's entire path of touches and calculates, four different ways, how much credit each channel should get for that conversion: crediting only the very first touch, only the very last touch, splitting credit evenly across every touch in the path, or weighting credit toward whichever touches happened closer to the actual conversion. Separately — and this is the more important half of the analysis — for specific stretches of time, marketing was genuinely and deliberately withheld from a portion of prospects in the paid and community channels, creating a real controlled comparison group. Comparing what happened to that group against a similar group that kept receiving normal marketing measures the true, cause-and-effect impact of that channel's marketing, rather than just which channel a customer happened to pass through on the way to converting.

## Key decisions and why

- **Credit-based attribution and cause-and-effect impact are reported side by side, never blended into one number.** They answer two genuinely different questions — which channel a conversion's history suggests gets credit, versus which channel's marketing actually caused a conversion that wouldn't otherwise have happened — and combining them into a single "adjusted" figure would produce a number that honestly answers neither question.
- **Four different crediting rules are calculated and compared, rather than settling on one and presenting it as the answer.** This shows leadership how much the answer to "which channel deserves credit" actually depends on the counting method chosen, rather than presenting one method's output as a fixed truth.
- **The rule that weights credit toward more recent touches uses a recency window set from how long this business's own deals typically take to close, not a generic industry-standard rate.** A generic default would have nearly erased credit for the community channel, whose typical path to conversion runs far longer than the other two — the window needs to reflect this business's real buying pattern, not a one-size-fits-all convention.
- **A more advanced statistical technique for estimating channel credit was deliberately not used in place of the four rule-based methods.** A real controlled test already exists for two of the three channels — marketing was genuinely switched off for a held-out group of prospects during specific stretches of time — so a modeled estimate of the same effect would be a step down from measuring it directly, not an improvement on it.
- **A model that tries to predict conversion likelihood from a prospect's touch pattern was also set aside.** How much marketing contact a prospect received is itself already a downstream consequence of whether that prospect was heading toward converting, so a model trained on touch volume would mostly be reading back a pattern already built into the funnel rather than surfacing an independent early signal.
- **Once a group of prospects is assigned to the reduced-marketing test group, it stays in that group for the analysis even if a few of them later pick up some incidental marketing contact anyway.** Reassigning prospects after the fact based on what actually happened to them would let the group boundary shift with the outcome, which would make the measured effect look better than the test can honestly support. Keeping the original assignment gives a real, if slightly conservative, answer.
- **A test period's result is only used once that period has had enough time for its outcome to fully play out, not as soon as early results start coming in.** Reading a test too early makes prospects who simply haven't had time to convert yet look identical to prospects who genuinely never will, which would hide the very difference the test exists to measure. Any period that hasn't finished playing out yet is reported as still in progress rather than folded into the answer early.
- **The three channels this analysis works with — organic content, paid, and community/events — aren't a choice made by this analysis.** They're the three channels the company's own growth framework already defines marketing's contribution as; this analysis is what makes that existing breakdown computable for the first time, not what decided what the breakdown should be.
- **Every number this analysis's math depends on is checked against a fresh, independent calculation run straight from the underlying records, not against a related number produced through the same calculation path.** A check that would pass regardless of whether the underlying figures were actually correct creates false confidence rather than real assurance, so the checks that matter here are built to genuinely be able to fail.

## What drives the result

- **Every individual marketing touchpoint** a prospect has, tracked at the raw event level rather than summarized in advance — website visits, ad clicks, event registrations, and similar interactions
- **The three sub-channels**: organic content, paid advertising, and community/events
- **Four ways of assigning credit for a conversion**: crediting the first touch, crediting the last touch, splitting credit evenly across the whole path, or weighting credit toward touches closer to conversion (using a roughly three-week recency window drawn from this business's own typical time-to-convert)
- **A real controlled test**: specific stretches of time during which paid and community marketing was deliberately withheld from a portion of prospects, creating a genuine comparison group against prospects who kept receiving normal marketing

## Current result

As of December 2025, looking across roughly 2,061 converted prospects: crediting only the first touch, organic content gets about 49% of the credit, paid about 38%, and community about 13%. Switching to a different crediting rule moves the answer only modestly — about 1.5% of all credit changes hands depending which of the four rules is used — which turns out to matter far less than a second, more fundamental question.

That second question is what the controlled test answers, and it tells a materially different story: paid marketing's true, cause-and-effect contribution is meaningfully smaller than the credit-based methods suggest — the credit-based read overstates paid's real impact by roughly 35% compared to what the tested group's actual behavior shows. Community shows a similar pattern (roughly 18% overstated), though that individual reading rests on too small a sample to trust on its own; only the combined result across both tested channels is on solid statistical footing. Organic content — the single largest channel by credit under every one of the four rules — cannot be tested this way at all, because there's no practical way to switch organic content off for a chosen group of prospects the way a paid ad budget or a community program can be. That's a structural limit of this kind of test, not a gap in what was built.

Underneath the same numbers, this analysis also shows, for the first time, that the cost of winning a customer varies enormously by channel in a way the single blended marketing-cost figure hides entirely — organic content is by far the cheapest per customer won, paid is markedly the most expensive, and community sits in between.

## Known limitations

- **One pattern in the underlying data made the community channel look like it disproportionately "closes" deals that other channels start.** That pattern was investigated and traced to how cross-channel touches happen to get distributed in the underlying records, not to any real difference in how prospects actually behave. It's reported here plainly as a limitation of what this particular dataset can show, rather than dressed up as a genuine insight about channel behavior.
- **Organic content's true causal contribution cannot be measured at all**, for a structural reason rather than an analytical shortfall: there is no practical way to run a controlled test on organic content the way one can be run on a paid or community budget, so the single largest channel by credited conversions has no cause-and-effect read to compare against.
- **The dollar return figures reported for each channel reflect only the deals that actually closed, not the complete set of deals a channel helped generate.** A deal that didn't close currently can't be traced back to the marketing touches that originally sourced it, so what's labeled "pipeline return" here is really "return on the deals that were won" — a real number, but a narrower one than the label might suggest.
