# Scenario Planning

## What this is

A calculator for "what if" questions about the operating scorecard. It answers a different question than the forecast does: not "what will actually happen," but "if one specific input changed — win rate, onboarding touch volume, account manager touchpoints, whatever it is — what would that do to the metrics leadership already watches, given the real math the business uses to compute them today." It's built for anyone in leadership planning who wants to pressure-test an assumption before it becomes a plan, without doing the tree math by hand or guessing at the ripple effects.

## Why it exists

The metric tree is built so every top-line number is the exact mathematical result of the smaller pieces beneath it — a sum, a product, or a ratio, not a loose association. The variance-diagnostic engine already uses that structure to explain misses after the fact, tracing a real result back down to its cause. This tool runs the same structure the other direction: forward, from a hypothetical instead of a real outcome, so a proposed change to any one input can be tested against the same real equations before anyone commits to it, rather than leadership estimating the ripple effect by intuition or a napkin calculation.

## How it works

A hypothetical starts as one plain statement — a specific input moves by a specific amount, for example "win rate improves five points" or "account manager touch volume drops ten percent." The tool takes the real, most recently completed month's numbers exactly as they stand today, applies only that stated change, and then walks the result up through the same real formulas that already produce the live scorecard — the identical sums, products, and ratios, not an approximation of them — all the way up to the top-line growth, efficiency, and durability metrics. Several hypothetical changes can be tested together in one pass, and where two of them ultimately affect the same underlying dollar figure, the tool resolves that down to one true combined effect rather than adding two separate estimates together, which would risk double-counting and produce a wrong answer wherever the real math isn't simple addition. Wherever a requested change sits on a part of the framework that genuinely can't be tied through to a top-line number with the data that exists today, the tool says so plainly rather than inventing a figure to fill the gap.

## Key decisions and why

- **The tool reuses the variance-diagnostic engine's own real numbers and tree structure directly, rather than building a second, separate copy of either.** This guarantees the starting point for every hypothetical is provably identical to what the variance-diagnostic engine and the weekly readout already show leadership — two tools describing "where things stand today" can never quietly disagree with each other.
- **A direct, deterministic calculation was used instead of a fitted statistical model.** The relationships this tool walks through are exact arithmetic identities already proven to hold in the real numbers, not patterns to be estimated from history. Fitting a model to approximate a relationship that's already known exactly would trade a correct answer for a noisy guess, so a straightforward recalculation of the real formulas is the accurate choice, not a simplified stand-in for something more sophisticated.
- **The set of inputs a hypothetical can be applied to is fixed to the metric tree's own real building blocks, not left open to any number someone might propose.** Every testable input is a real, already-measured quantity the business tracks today — nothing is invented for the tool's convenience — so a result can always be traced back to something genuinely in the operating data.
- **Where a piece of the framework can't be tied through to a top-line number with the data that exists today, the tool reports that plainly instead of guessing.** A handful of paths through the tree — how new customer revenue ties to pipeline, win rate, and deal size together; how expansion revenue ties to wallet-share growth and overage together; and the two efficiency ratios that would need rep cost data no source in the business currently captures — don't hold together as clean, provable math yet. Rather than force a number through anyway, the tool names the gap directly and still reports whatever real, different consequence of the same change does compute.
- **When two or more hypothetical changes affect the same underlying dollar figure, the tool combines them into one true value instead of stacking separate estimates on top of each other.** Two ways of describing the same change — for example, naming it directly versus naming it as a share of starting revenue — are recognized as the same real quantity; if they're given conflicting values in one request, the tool flags the conflict rather than picking one arbitrarily.
- **A hypothetical is applied only to the most recently completed month, not projected forward across future months.** Every prior month feeding a trailing calculation, like retention measured across the past year, is left at its real, actual value. This keeps the question well-defined as "what if this one month were different," rather than quietly becoming a forecast of how long a change would persist — that longer-horizon question belongs to the forecast tool, not this one.
- **Whole-number inputs, like a count of account manager touchpoints, are rounded to the nearest real whole number rather than left as a fraction.** These are genuine headcount-style counts, not divisible quantities, so a requested change that would otherwise imply a fraction of a touchpoint or a fraction of an account is rounded before it's reported or applied — a small, deliberate precision trade-off rather than a display glitch.

## What drives the result

- **The real, current-month baseline** — reused directly from the same up-to-date numbers the variance-diagnostic engine already reports, so every hypothetical starts from the same ground truth leadership already sees
- **What can be tested** — a fixed list of thirteen real inputs spanning growth, efficiency, and durability (things like win rate, average deal size, expansion/contraction/churn dollars, account manager touch volume, automated usage volume, acquisition cost, and the account counts behind logo retention), plus five additional ways of naming a change in rate terms (for example, as a share of starting revenue) that resolve back to three of those same thirteen underlying dollar figures
- **How the change is stated** — as a brand-new value, a plus-or-minus amount, or a percentage move, whichever is the most natural way to describe the hypothetical in question
- **The point in time tested** — the most recently completed month only; every earlier month inside a trailing calculation stays at its real, unperturbed value
- **Known gaps** — a small, named set of paths through the framework that can't be tied through to a real top-line number with today's data, reported honestly as "can't compute this" rather than filled with a guess

## Current result

As of the most recent validation checkpoint (December 31, 2025, with an earlier check confirmed again at June 30, 2025), the tool's starting numbers were confirmed to match the variance-diagnostic engine's own real, live numbers exactly, for every metric this tool covers — proof that the two tools' shared starting point genuinely holds rather than merely being assumed. All seven of the tool's built-in known-answer test cases passed at both checkpoints, covering every kind of relationship the tree contains (a sum, a product, a ratio, a shared input reached by more than one path with no double-counting, and both of the honest "can't compute this" cases).

As a real illustration of the tool in use: run against the real December 2025 numbers with two hypothetical changes at once — Commercial contraction dollars fifteen percent higher, and Enterprise account manager touchpoints ten percent lower — fifteen downstream metrics computed cleanly with no gaps hit. The contraction-and-churn figure moved from roughly $1.18 million to roughly $1.22 million, an increase of about $36,000, exactly matching the size of the Commercial contraction change on its own; the same $36,000 flowed through to the company-wide growth bridge figure in the opposite direction, as the real formula requires; and net revenue retention shifted only slightly, from roughly 1.915 to roughly 1.908, since only one of the twelve months inside that trailing calculation was touched by the hypothetical.

## Known limitations

- **Only inputs with a genuine, provable path to a top-line number can be tested end to end.** A handful of paths through the framework — how new customer revenue ties to pipeline, win rate, and deal size together; how expansion revenue ties to wallet-share growth and overage together; and the two cost-dependent efficiency ratios — don't hold together as real, tie-able math with the data that exists today. A hypothetical touching one of these gets an honest "can't compute this" for the blocked figure specifically, alongside whatever real, different consequence of the same change does compute.
- **When a hypothetical changes win rate or average deal size, the overall company-wide growth figure is reported with new customer revenue held at its real, unchanged number and specifically flagged as incomplete.** This is a stated limit of what this tool can prove, not a claim that new customer revenue genuinely wouldn't move under that hypothetical.
- **Whole-number inputs are rounded to the nearest real count**, which means a requested change that would otherwise land on a fraction is nudged slightly to the nearest whole number — a small, real precision effect specific to count-based inputs like touchpoint volume or account counts, not present on dollar or rate-based inputs.
- **A hypothetical only describes one month at a time.** It can't yet express a change that persists across several future months — that longer-horizon question is what the forecast tool is built to answer, not this one.
- **Enterprise win rate and average deal size can come back as "not available" in some months**, specifically when there simply were no won Enterprise deals that month to compute a real number from. This is reported honestly as a genuine data-sparsity fact rather than filled in or hidden.
- **No new data was added anywhere in the business's systems to build this tool.** Every input and formula it uses already existed in the built scorecard and in the variance-diagnostic engine's own prior work.
